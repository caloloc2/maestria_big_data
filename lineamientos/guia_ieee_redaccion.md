## I. INTRODUCTION This style manual provides general editing guidelines for IEEE Magazines. For guidance in grammar and usage not included in this manual, please consult the most recent version of the Chicago Manual of Style , published by the University of Chicago Press. The IEEE’s responsibility in editing articles is not to make any determination on or do any editing of the technical content we work with, but is instead to render the work as readable, grammatically correct, and as consistent with IEEE style as possible.

## II. STYLE AND USAGE

A. CAPITALIZATION
1) Headlines and Subheads We follow the “headline style” in The Chicago Manual of Style, except that prepositions of three letters or fewer should be lowercase in heads and subheads: Time-Frequency Analysis of Heart-Rate Variability Playing With Fire and Without Matches Better Protection for Improved Detection 
-  All verbs should be initial capped (note: in infinitives, “to” is not initial capped). 
-  Articles (a, an, the) and coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, for, nor, ...) of three letters or fewer should be lowercase. 
-  Subordinating conjunctions (if, because, as, that ...) are initial capped. 
-  The second word in a hyphenated compound is capitalized. 
-  All words of four letters or more are capped.
2) Corporate and Academic Titles Titles should be lowercase unless directly preceding a person’s name, except for named academic professorships/fellowships: John Smith, professor of engineering, wrote the article. The article was submitted by Prof. John Smith.

## III. NUMBERS

A. WHEN TO SPELL OUT, WHEN TO USE NUMERALS
1) Spell out all numbers nine and under; use numerals for numbers 10 and above: There were five volunteers from Region 9 and 15 volunteers from Region 8 at the meeting. The board contained two DSPs, nine capacitors, and 13 transistors. In a table, if the data are all numerals, all numbers (even those nine and below) should remain numerals.
2) Always spell out the number when it is the first word in a sentence or rework the sentence (for large numbers it may be best to rewrite the sentence so that it doesn’t begin with the number): Forty-five IEEE Fellows were recognized during the awards ceremony.

3) For ordinals, the same rules apply (use numerals for 10 and above): The eighth trial yielded the desired results. She placed 11th out of 200 students
4) Use numbers when abbreviations/symbols are used for units of measure: Ex: 3 mm

B. PERCENTAGES AND DECIMALS
1) Always use the number and the percent sign when dealing with percentages. The percentage symbol is repeated in lists and ranges. Only 2% of the transformers failed the test. The students made up 20%–30% of the population.
2) When using decimal fractions in text, include the zero before decimal if needed for clarity; otherwise omit it. Do not include the zero(s) after the last digit following a decimal. .25 0.8

C. COMMA USAGE
1) In text and figure labels, use a comma to indicate thousand, ten thousand, and hundred thousand: 1,000 22,000
2) For millions and billions, use the following format: 1 billion people There were 2 million apes affected by the virus .

D. MONETARY UNITS
1) Use symbols for units of currency with numerals: Members can subscribe to the magazine for US$125.50. The book was priced at CAD$43.
2) For whole dollar amounts, do not use .00 after the last numeral, and delete it if included: The price of the book was US$25 .

E. IEEE DIVISIONS AND REGIONS
1) IEEE Divisions are identified by Roman numerals: IEEE Circuits and Devices Magazine is sponsored by three Societies within Division I .
2) IEEE Regions are identified by Arabic numerals: Regions 2,4, and 9 experienced dramatic membership gains .

H. CONTINUOUS NUMBERS
1) For continuous/inclusive numbers, use an en dash: see pages 26–29 The war occurred during the 1893–1901 time period . However, use “to” with “from” and “and” with “between”: The war lasted from 1910 to 1912. The stray cat was between 12 and 15 years old . Note: There is an exception: References in text will not include an en dash. All references will be written out. [1]–[4] will now be [1], [2], [3], [4].
2) If numerals are missing, insert the missing digits: pp. 127–39 becomes pp. 127–139 1987–89 becomes 1987–1989

## V. ABBREVIATIONS, ACRONYMS, AND SYMBOLS
1) ACRONYMS
1) Spell out (define) terms upon first use and follow with the acronym in parentheses. The acronym should be used throughout remainder of article: The digital signal processor (DSP) transformed the field. The acronym must be used to define one term, not two. For example, DSP cannot stand for digital signal processor and digital signal processing in the same article. If the acronym is only used once in the article, the term is spelled out and the acronym deleted. Some acronyms are so common within a field that they do not need to be defined; if an author states this, leave as is. Spell out acronyms in titles of articles without providing an acronym, unless authors strongly object. Note: DO NOT spell out the acronyms LGBTQIA+, LGBT, LGBTQ, LGBTQIA.
2) Add an “s” to the acronym in plural constructions (even if the first use of the term is plural). If the acronym is used singular afterward, drop the “s.” If the plural form of the word itself does not contain an “s” continue to add one to the acronym: Their digital signal processors (DSPs) were the quickest. The Xylink DSP was a close second .

## VI. FIGURES, TABLES, AND SIDEBARS Authors may include appendixes in their articles, which should be reformatted as figures, tables, or sidebars, whichever is most appropriate.

A. FIGURES
1) Figures should appear as close to their citation in text as possible.
2) When referencing figures in text, always spell out the word Figure and follow it with the figure number: The transistor diagram (Figure
3) indicates ... Figure 9 illustrates the power supply connections for the device.
3) When mentioning parts of the figure, include the letter in parentheses: Figure 9(d) shows the completed chip.
4) When referring to multiple parts of the same figure, treat Figure as singular as it is one figure with multiple parts: Each step of the design process is shown in Figure 9(a)–(d). Figure 10(a) and (b) shows the two option s.

B. CAPTIONS
1) Figure and table captions should begin with FIGURE #. and TABLE #. and the caption should be in sentence case, followed by ending punctuation. FIGURE 1. The flowchart is shown. TABLE 2. Examples of chemical reactions.
2) For captions of figures with multiple parts, place labels in parentheses: FIGURE 1. (a) The flowchart of the simulation process. (b) The simulation display.
3) Be consistent throughout the issue in how parts of a figure are punctuated. Note that the label is placed before the descriptive text, not after it: 
-  Correct: The gradual transition between stages. The transition begins at (a) stage one, (b) stage two, and (c) stage three. 
-  Wrong: The gradual transition between stages. The transition begins at stage one (a), stage two (b), and stage three (c).
4) Acronyms that are defined in a figure can be placed at the end of the caption, before any source information, and should follow this format: PET: positron emission tomography; ADC: analog-to-digital converter. Note that this same format can be used when defining acronyms in a table footnote.
5) Be consistent with how names are treated in figure captions. Use person’s full name; after that, just use the last name unless expansion is needed for clarity. Use a title (such as Prof. or Dr.) if given. Mr., Mrs., Miss, and Ms. are not used. When multiple people are named in a photo, use terms such as (from left), (center), (second from right) to identify them.

-  In general, try to duplicate the spacing, indentation, punctuation, and capitalization in the author’s manuscript. Programming languages don’t normally recognize boldface or italics, but the author might use them for emphasis. Spacing is very important because omitting a space will often prevent a computer from recognizing a command or variable. Indentations often indicate subsets of the program. Although they aren’t necessary to the computer, they make a program easier to understand.

B. PUNCTUATION 
-  Don’t substitute two single typesetting quotation marks (‘ ’) or normal double typesetting quotation marks (“ ”) for typewriter quotation marks (").

-  Don’t substitute a raised asterisk (*) for a multiplication asterisk (*).

C. CAPITALIZATION Words or letters in programming languages generally represent commands (tokens) or variables. In Fortran and Basic, the general convention is to capitalize commands (such as IF, PRINT, FORMAT, GOTO) and variables (often I, N, X(n.)). In C, Algol, and PostScript, these words and letters are lowercase.

Case sensitive ABC, C, C++, Eiffel, Interlisp, Loops (including the Common Loops and Concurrent Loops dialects), Modula-2, Prolog, and Smalltalk.
